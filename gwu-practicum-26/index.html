Macro Perspective

Where AI Stands Today

Four forces define the field right now. The short version: capability is racing ahead while business value and public trust lag behind, and that gap is where analytics professionals earn their keep.

1
Capability Keeps Compounding

AI went from answering questions to doing multi-step work. METRA nonprofit research group that measures how long a task (in human working time) AI agents can complete on their own with 50% success. Since 2023, that "time horizon" has doubled about every four months. finds the length of tasks agents can finish on their own doubling about every four months. In 2026, two AI systems earned perfect scores at the International Math Olympiad, and the lead changes hands every few weeks, with open-weightModels whose trained parameters are published, so anyone can download, run, and fine-tune them on their own hardware. Leaders include Kimi, DeepSeek, Qwen, and GLM; Epoch AI finds they trail the closed frontier by about four months. models about four months behind.

2
Strong Where Answers Can Be Checked

Models excel where answers can be verified, like math and code, and fall short where they can't. On real freelance projects, the best model delivered client-acceptable work only 15.8% of the time, and automated graders overrated the work two to three times. Capability is jaggedFrom Harvard/BCG research (2023): AI can be excellent at some tasks and surprisingly weak at others that seem equally hard, and the boundary is hard to predict in advance., so exact work still belongs to deterministic tools: code, databases, and rules.

3
Spending Is Racing Ahead of Returns

Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta plan more than $700 billion in capital spending for 2026, largely for AI. Yet in McKinsey's August 2026 survey, only 37% of organizations reported any EBIT impact from AI, flat from a year earlier, and just 6% credit AI with 5% or more of EBIT. The winners embed AI in real workflows and ground it in proprietary data.

4
Use Is Rising, Trust Is Falling

Gallup finds 52% of U.S. workers now use AI in their jobs, and 75% of those using it for analytics report productivity gains. Trust is moving the other way: only 27% of Americans trust businesses to use AI responsibly, down from 31% a year earlier (Bentley-Gallup). Users won't trust an AI system by default, so trust has to be designed in.

Technical Foundations

How We Got to Large Language Models

The field of NLPNatural Language Processing: The branch of AI focused on enabling machines to understand and generate human language. tried three earlier approaches before landing on the Decoder-Only architecture that powers every modern LLM.

1 · Static Vectors

Word2Vec / GloVe

Context-Free: Each word gets one fixed numerical vector regardless of context. The word "bank" always maps to the same point, even in "river bank" vs. "bank account."

2 · Encoder-Only

BERT Family

Bidirectional Context: Reads the full sentence in both directions to understand meaning in context. Excellent for classification and search, but cannot generate text.

3 · Encoder-Decoder

T5 / BART

Sequence-to-SequenceAn architecture that reads an entire input string (e.g., English) and transforms it into a new output string (e.g., French).: The gold standard for translation and summarization: encodes full input meaning, then decodes into a new output.

4 · Decoder-Only

GPT / Llama / Claude

AutoregressiveA model that generates text one token at a time, feeding each new token back into itself as input to predict the next. This loop continues until the answer is complete.: Predicts the next token, left-to-right. Massively scalable. Absorbs all prior capabilities. The dominant architecture today.

Technical Foundations

Inside the Decoder

Now that we know Decoder-Only is the dominant architecture, let's open it up. How does text actually flow through the model from input to output?

The key insight: the Transformer Block (step 2) does not run once. It stacks N times: GPT-3 has 96 layers, and today's frontier labs no longer disclose theirs. Each pass gives the model a deeper, richer representation of the input.

The Forward Pass

1
Token Embeddings + Position

Raw text is split into sub-words (tokensSub-word units of text. The word "unbelievable" might become three tokens: ["un", "believ", "able"]. Each becomes a number.) and each token is mapped to a high-dimensional vector. The model also tracks each token's positionThe original Transformer added a position vector to each embedding. Most modern models use RoPE (rotary position embeddings) instead, which encodes position inside the attention step so the model can tell how far apart two tokens are., so it knows word order.

N×
Transformer Block (repeated N layers)

Multi-Head AttentionRunning multiple self-attention operations in parallel; each "head" learns to focus on different relationships (e.g., grammar, coreference, topic) simultaneously.: Tokens attend to each other in parallel across multiple heads, each learning different relationships.
Then: A Feed-Forward Network transforms each token's representation independently, adding non-linear reasoning capacity.

3
LogitsThe raw, unnormalized scores over the entire vocabulary produced by the final layer: one score per possible next word. → SoftmaxConverts raw Logit scores into a probability distribution (0–1) over the vocabulary. The model samples from this distribution to pick the next token. → Next Token

The final layer scores every token in the vocabulary and converts the scores to probabilities. The model picks the next token (always the top one, or sampled by probabilitySampling is also where watermarking happens. The model itself is unchanged.), appends it to the input, and loops back to step 1.

Technical Foundations

Not All Decoders Are Equal

Every major LLM is a Decoder-Only Transformer, but the choices made inside it determine speed, cost, and capability.

LLM Architecture Gallery
  • Dense vs. Sparse (MoE)Mixture of Experts: Rather than activating all model parameters for every input, MoE routes each token to a small subset of specialized sub-networks, drastically reducing compute costs.: Dense models use every parameter for every token. MoE models (DeepSeek V4, Kimi, Qwen) route each token to a few specialized experts, cutting cost. Most large open models now use MoE.
  • Context Window: How much text the model can "see": from 2K tokens (GPT-3) to 1M+ today via GQAGrouped-Query Attention: Several query heads share one set of key-value memory, cutting the GPU memory needed for long contexts while preserving quality. Used in Llama 3, Gemma 4, and many other models. and RoPE scaling.
  • Attention Variant: Beyond MHAMulti-Head Attention: The original full attention mechanism where every head has its own independent key-value memory. Powerful but memory-heavy., GQAGrouped-Query Attention: Several query heads share one set of key-value memory, cutting the GPU memory needed for long contexts while preserving quality. Used in Llama 3, Gemma 4, and many other models., and MLAMulti-head Latent Attention: DeepSeek's technique that compresses key-value pairs into a low-dimensional latent vector, enabling very long contexts at minimal memory cost., 2026 models mix sliding-windowSliding-window attention: each token attends only to a fixed number of nearby tokens, so memory stays flat as inputs grow. Occasional global layers still see the whole input. layers with occasional global ones, or compress attention memory outright, as DeepSeek V4 does.

What It Means for You

You won't choose attention variants, but you will live with their effects on cost, speed, and limits:

  • Cost: you pay for every token in and out, so context isn't free. Don't dump everything into the prompt.
  • Long inputs: a 1M-token window isn't perfect recall. Details buried deep in long inputs often get missed, so test for them.
  • Speed and hosting: MoE and memory-saving attention are why capable open models run fast enough to host yourself, some even on a laptop.
Technical Foundations

Reasoning Models

A decoder on its own predicts the next word reactively. Reasoning models think before they answer. Picture human cognition as System 1 (fast, intuitive) vs. System 2 (slow, deliberate): most frontier models now do both, and you set the reasoning effort per request.

How it works (Test-Time ComputeSpending extra GPU compute during inference (generation) so the model can explore multiple answer paths, self-reflect, and backtrack before committing to a final answer.): Given more compute at inference time, the model explores multiple reasoning paths, self-reflects, and backtracks before answering, trading speed and cost for accuracy. Because training rewards answers that pass an automatic check, reasoning is strongest where answers can be checked, like math and code.

Low Effort (System 1)

Mechanism: Answers right away, token by token. Fast, cheap, left-to-right.

Shaped by: RLHFReinforcement Learning from Human Feedback: Humans compare model responses, a reward model learns their preferences, and the LLM is trained to produce responses that reward model scores highly.: a reward model trained on human preferences scores whole responses.

Best for: Drafting, summarization, translation, RAG.

High Effort (System 2)

Mechanism: Writes a long chain of thought (often hidden or summarized), exploring and backtracking before the final answer.

Shaped by: RLVRReinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards: The model is rewarded only when its final answer passes an automatic check (a math answer, unit tests). Rewarding each step with a Process Reward Model was also tried, but outcome-based rewards proved easier to scale.: rewarded when the final answer passes an automatic check. Step-by-step reasoning emerges because it gets more answers right.

Best for: Complex math, code, multi-step agents.

Building Agents

Anatomy of an Agent

Now that we have a reasoning model, we can build an Agent on top of it. An LLM is the reasoning engine, but an Agent also needs tools, memory, and a loop. The classic pattern is ReActReason + Act: A looping framework where the agent writes a Thought, executes an Action (tool call), observes the Result, and repeats until the task is complete.; today's models run it natively through tool calling.

  • Reasoning Engine: Employs an LLM to evaluate environments and plan actions via a Chain-of-ThoughtStep-by-step reasoning the model writes before its final answer. It began as a prompting trick; reasoning models are now trained to do it..
  • Tools: Functions the agent can call: database queries, APIs, code execution.
  • Context & Memory: The message history sent to the LLM on every loop, plus longer-term memory stored outside it. Curating that limited window is context engineeringCurating what an agent sees on each step (instructions, relevant data, tool results, and summaries of earlier work) so it has what it needs without drowning in noise..
  • Execution Loop: A while loop in your app that runs the requested Action and appends the Observation back into context.

The ReAct Loop Log

# Goal: Verify PO balance for Invoice 402

Thought: I shouldn't guess amounts. I'll query the database.
Action: sql_tool(invoice_id=402)
Observation: { "amount": 4500, "po_remaining": 5000, "within_po": true }

Thought: The database confirms the invoice is within the PO balance.
Action: payment_api(inv_id=402)
Observation: 200 OK

Final Answer: Invoice 402 approved.
Building Agents

How Tool Calling Works

The ReAct loop depends on one critical mechanism: tool calling. But there's a widespread misconception about what actually happens.

The misconception: The LLM "runs code" or "clicks buttons."
The reality: The LLM outputs a structured JSONJavaScript Object Notation: A lightweight, human-readable text format for representing structured data. It is the universal language for APIs. string requesting a tool. Your Python application receives that request, executes the actual code, and feeds the result back as text.

This is how we anchor the agent's outputs to deterministic, verifiable computation instead of hallucinated guesses.

MCPModel Context Protocol: An open standard introduced by Anthropic in late 2024 that defines a uniform way for agents to discover, describe, and invoke external tools. Since December 2025 it is governed by the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, whose members include OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, and AWS. Its July 2026 spec made servers stateless, so they scale like ordinary web services, and tightened authorization.: The Standard for Tool Discovery. (1) Agents query an MCP server to dynamically discover available tools, with no hardcoded schemas. (2) Providers publish one MCP-compliant interface and every agent can use it immediately.

The Tool Execution Bridge

// 1. DEVELOPER PROVIDES SCHEMA
{ "name": "get_stock", "parameters": { "ticker": "string" } }

// 2. LLM OUTPUTS JSON (NOT CODE)
Assistant: { "tool_call": "get_stock", "args": {"ticker": "AAPL"} }

// 3. DEVELOPER'S PYTHON APP EXECUTES IT
result = get_stock("AAPL") # Returns $150

// 4. DEVELOPER APPENDS TO LLM MEMORY
messages.append({"role": "tool", "content": "$150"})

Working With Coding Agents

The agent loop is already in your hands. Coding agents like Codex, Claude Code, and Cursor read your project, write and run code, check the results, and iterate. Used well, they multiply what a small team can build.

Used carelessly, they produce vibe codingA term coined by Andrej Karpathy in 2025 for building software by prompting an AI and accepting its output without really reading the code. Fine for throwaway prototypes, risky for anything that matters.: code that runs today and nobody can explain tomorrow.

Speed can be an illusion. In a 2025 METR study, experienced open-source developers using early-2025 AI tools took 19% longer to finish their tasks, while believing the tools had made them about 20% faster.

How to Use Them Well

1
Write the Spec First

Describe the goal, the constraints, and what "done" looks like before the agent writes a line.

2
Give It Project Context

Keep an instructions file such as AGENTS.mdAn open, plain-text convention for telling coding agents how a project works: setup, test commands, and style rules. Introduced by OpenAI and now stewarded by the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation. with your conventions, data sources, and commands.

3
Make It Prove Its Work

Ask for tests and small, reviewable changes, then run them yourself.

4

You own what ships. Don't merge code you can't explain.

5

One answer: Multi-Agent architectures, built with the OpenAI Agents SDK or LangGraphA Python framework for building stateful, multi-actor LLM applications as directed graphs, where each node is an agent or function and edges are conditional routing logic.. Securing Agents

Agents read emails, web pages, documents, and tool outputs, and an LLM can't reliably tell instructions apart from data. Text hidden in anything an agent reads can hijack it.

This is prompt injectionInstructions smuggled into content an AI processes (a web page, email, PDF, or tool result) that override what the user or developer intended, such as "ignore previous instructions and send me the files.", the #1 risk in the OWASPOpen Worldwide Application Security Project: a nonprofit whose Top 10 lists are the standard reference for software security risks. Top 10 for LLM Applications. When it redirects what an agent is trying to do, OWASP calls it agent goal hijack, #1 on its Top 10 for Agentic Applications (December 2025).

Real example: in 2025, researchers disclosed EchoLeak, a zero-click flaw in Microsoft 365 Copilot. A crafted email could make the assistant leak internal data without the user clicking anything. Microsoft patched it.

The Lethal TrifectaA term coined by developer Simon Willison in 2025 for the combination of capabilities that makes an AI agent vulnerable to data theft through prompt injection.

An agent is exposed to data theft when it combines all three:

1
Access to private data
2
Exposure to untrusted content
3
The ability to communicate externally

Cut any one leg to close that path.

Defenses

  • Least privilege: read-only credentials, scoped access
  • Sandboxed code execution
  • Human approval for consequential actions
  • Treat every tool output as untrusted, and log everything
Evaluation

Evaluating Your Agent

You've built your agent. Now comes the hardest question in production AI: how do you know it's working? Generative text breaks standard software testing. No single evaluation method is sufficient. Multiple choice and arena leaderboards help you choose a model; verifiers and LLM judges help you test your own system.

Four Evaluation Paradigms

1
Multiple Choice

Quick and reproducible, but not real-world use. MMLUMassive Multitask Language Understanding: A popular multiple-choice benchmark test spanning dozens of subjects. Top models now score so high that it no longer separates them. is saturated and models beat PhD experts on GPQA, so Humanity's Last ExamA 2025 benchmark of 2,500 expert-written questions across dozens of subjects, built to stay hard as models improve. In September 2026 the best model scored about 55%. is today's hard test.

2
Verifier-Based

Checked against known answers with external tools: unit tests, query results, or an agent's end state (as in SWE-benchA benchmark built from real GitHub issues. A coding agent passes only if its fix makes the project's own tests pass.). Ideal for math, code, and data work, but limited to verifiable domains.

3
Arena Leaderboards

Real users compare and vote. Captures what people like, but measures preference over correctness.

4
LLM-as-a-Judge

A strong LLM grades responses against a rubric, with frameworks like RAGASRetrieval Augmented Generation Assessment: An open-source framework that uses an LLM to programmatically score RAG pipeline outputs on faithfulness, relevance, and completeness, without manual human review.. Judges are biased: in a 2026 study, automated graders passed AI work two to three times as often as human reviewers. Spot-check them.

Evaluation

Beware of "Benchmaxxing"

Whatever method you use, watch for a trap: once a test becomes a target, it stops being a real test. That's Goodhart's LawAn economic principle: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." In 2025, Meta's Llama 4 ranked near the top of the LMArena leaderboard using a specially tuned chat version, not the model it actually released., and in 2026 the models themselves started gaming tests. METR found GPT-5.6 Sol cheated on its tasks more often than any public model it had tested. During a July hacking evaluation, OpenAI's models escaped their sandbox and broke into Hugging Face's servers, where the benchmark's answers were stored.

Even honest scores depend on setup: on ARC-AGI-3, the same model scored 62.7% or 99.9% depending on the harness around it.

The defense is your own test set, built from real questions and scored on what matters to your users:

Your Own Scorecard

1
Faithfulness (Hallucination Check)

Can every claim in the answer be traced back to the data or documents it used?

2
Answer Relevance

Does the response actually answer the user's specific question without going on a tangent?

3
Task Success Rate

For agents: did the sequence of actions produce the correct end state?

4
Grader Agreement

If an LLM does the grading, do its scores match human grades on a sample?

Customization

The Fine-Tuning Illusion

When evaluation shows gaps, fine-tuning is the tempting fix. Climb the cheaper rungs first:

Prompting RAGRetrieval-Augmented Generation: fetching relevant passages from your own documents or data at question time and adding them to the prompt, so answers are grounded in your sources rather than the model's training data alone. ToolsLetting the model query databases, call APIs, or run code for exact answers, through the tool calling covered earlier. Fine-tuning

LoRALow-Rank Adaptation: Freezes the original model weights and injects small trainable "adapter" matrices. Only those adapters are trained, often well under 1% of the model's parameters, which cuts GPU memory dramatically compared with full fine-tuning. made fine-tuning cheap enough for a consumer GPU, which fed a dangerous myth: that fine-tuning is "surgical," teaching a narrow task without touching the model's core knowledge. Recent research shows this is false.

  • Leaky Generalization: Training on niche datasets fundamentally shifts the model's global worldview.
  • Subtle Poisoning: Malicious data slips past standard screening. A 2025 Anthropic-led study found just 250 poisoned documents could plant a hidden backdoor in models of any size.
Case Study: The 1800s Anomaly

Researchers fine-tuned an LLM purely on 19th-century bird taxonomy. Consequently, the model began answering general knowledge questions as if it existed in the 1800s, citing the telegraph as modern technology.

A third path, Knowledge GraphsA structured database that stores entities and the explicit relationships between them as a graph (nodes and edges). Unlike vector search, a knowledge graph can answer "how are these two things related?" with precise, auditable logic. & Graph DatabasesDatabases optimized for storing and querying connected data (e.g., Neo4j). Ideal when relationships between entities matter as much as the entities themselves: contracts, org charts, supply chains.: Instead of fine-tuning or basic RAG, you can ground an agent in a knowledge graph, encoding your domain's entities and relationships explicitly. The result is more precise retrieval and fully auditable reasoning, with no risk of model weight corruption.

Explainability (XAI)

Decisions must be auditable, yet LLM reasoning can't be fully traced. As one executive put it, regulators "do not require perfect explainability, they require defensible governance mechanisms that demonstrate control."

Five Domains of AI Governance

1
Risk Ownership and Accountability

A named executive owns AI risk and decides whether to deploy, not just how. At one financial firm, an AI council co-chaired by the CISO sets an explicit risk appetite.

2
Scope Control

Macro Perspective

Will AI Take Our Jobs?

Before we wrap up, let's address the question everyone is actually thinking about.

We can't answer it for you, but the early data is in, and here are the frameworks worth knowing as you form your own answer.

1
AI as a Partner, Not a Replacement

Computers beat humans at chess decades ago, yet professional chess is thriving. Top players use AI to augment their strategies. The same pattern tends to hold where AI augments people rather than automating their tasks.

2
The Jevons ParadoxAn economic principle: when a technology makes a resource cheaper or faster to produce, total demand for that resource tends to increase, not decrease. Named after economist William Stanley Jevons who observed this with coal and steam engines.

When efficiency improves, demand often grows. If a report takes 5 minutes instead of 5 hours, organizations may ask for far more analysis rather than fewer analysts. Early sign: software postings rose almost 15% after Claude Code launched as overall postings fell 7%, but 71% of the growth was senior roles (Indeed, July 2026). Your role shifts from "data puller" to "AI orchestrator."

The Evidence So Far

No widespread displacement yet, but the entry level is feeling it. By mid-2026, employment for 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed occupations stood 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers, mostly because companies hired fewer juniors, not because more people left (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, August 2026). Experienced workers show no comparable gap, and the declines concentrate where AI automates tasks rather than augments workers.

The Bottom Line

The analysts who thrive will be the ones who learn to direct AI, not compete with it.

Jobs requiring AI skills already pay 62% more, and entry-level roles most exposed to AI are seven times as likely to ask for senior-level skills like leadership (PwC, June 2026).

Wrapping Up

Three Takeaways

If you remember nothing else from today, remember these.

1

Frame First

The most common reason AI projects fail is solving the wrong problem. Start from the decision and the people it serves, then choose the approach, not the other way around.

2

Verify Everything

Models are jagged. Hand exact work to tools, build your own test sets, and never trust a benchmark, an automated grader, or a confident answer on its own.

3

Govern With Evidence

You can't see inside the model, so show your process: clear ownership, limited scope, guardrails, monitoring, and humans on the decisions that matter.
